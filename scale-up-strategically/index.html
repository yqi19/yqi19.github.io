Scale Up Strategically

Learning Compositional Generalization via Bias-Aware Evaluation and Data Collection for Robotic Manipulation

Where you spend demonstrations matters as much as how many you collect.

Paper arXiv Video Code soon Data soon BibTeX

TL;DR Fine-tuned robot policies latch onto some instruction factors, like color, and ignore others, like the verb. We measure this instruction factor bias across six foundation policies, find a hierarchy they largely share, and use it to decide where a fixed demonstration budget should go.

Motivation

Scaling up, but how?

Robots in the open world face an unlimited space of instructions. Objects, attributes, skills, and spatial relations combine in ways no dataset can enumerate, so a useful policy must generalize compositionally: it has to execute new combinations of familiar instruction factors that it never saw together during training.

Scaling up demonstrations has become the default recipe for generalization in robot learning: more data, more tasks, more embodiments. Yet this line of work mostly tells us to collect more data, not how to collect it. When the demonstration budget is limited, which combinations of objects, attributes, and skills should we collect?

We take a first step toward answering this question. Instead of the amount of data, we study the distribution of demonstrations across instruction factors, and show that it determines which factors a policy learns to ground. Diagnosing this bias tells us where the next demonstrations should go.

Abstract

Compositional generalization is essential for robots to follow diverse instructions. However, pretrained policies are known to take shortcuts, deferring to salient cues rather than grounding language. We introduce a diagnostic framework that localizes this failure to individual instruction factors, reusable semantic components such as color, verb, object, size, and spatial attribute. Our framework formalizes instruction factor bias, the tendency of fine-tuned policies to over-rely on dominant factors as shortcuts, and quantifies it through two metrics: the Factor Dominance Rate (FDR), capturing pairwise bias between factors, and the Factor Dominance Hierarchy (FDH), aggregating these into a global ranking. Across six foundation policies, color is the most dominant factor, and verb and size are the most under-grounded; five of the six follow the ordering color ≥ object ≥ spatial ≥ verb ≥ size. We further show the diagnosis is actionable: a bias-aware data collection strategy that reallocates a fixed budget toward under-grounded factors outperforms baselines under matched demonstration budgets in simulation and on a real robot.

Evaluation

Measuring which factors a policy listens to

STEP 1

Correlate two factors

Fine-tune on data where two factors always co-occur (blue cells), e.g. each verb is paired with one object.

STEP 2

Force a choice

Test on held-out combinations. The scene lets the policy follow either factor, but not both.

STEP 3

Score and rank

A VLM judge labels each rollout. FDR scores each pair; Copeland ranking aggregates pairs into the FDH.

With Nf₁ and Nf₂ the number of rollouts that overfit to each factor, FDR(f₁, f₂) = (Nf₁ − Nf₂) / (Nf₁ + Nf₂). Positive values mean f₁ dominates, negative values mean f₂ dominates, and values near 0 mean the two are balanced.

Method

Bias-aware data collection

The instruction space grows combinatorially with the number of factors, so covering every combination is rarely affordable, especially on a real robot. We keep the demonstration budget fixed and change where it goes.

V (ours) collects one column that fixes the under-grounded factor while varying the other, and adds the diagonal and subdiagonal. With three or more factors, it adds a column per under-grounded factor, in FDH order. We compare against L, which fixes each factor and samples the other uniformly, Random, which samples combinations uniformly, and Complete, which covers everything and serves as an upper-bound reference.

Because the hierarchy is largely shared, V uses one global FDH for all backbones rather than re-diagnosing each model.

Results

Simulation

We evaluate in ManiSkill with π0, π0.5, and GR00T-N1.7. Under matched factor change and demonstration budget, V matches or outperforms L and Random in the large majority of settings. The gains are largest when a strong factor dominates (+10 points on Color-Object, +7 on Color-Spatial) and when three or more factors vary. The main exception is Verb-Size on π0.5, where size remains hard to ground for every strategy.

The same global strategy also transfers to OpenVLA-oft, whose own hierarchy differs from the global one: V still outperforms both baselines in the Verb-Color-Object and All-Factor settings.

Real robot

On a UR5 with a RealSense D455 agent-view camera and a FLIR wrist camera, we fine-tune GR00T-N1.7 on three tasks. Each task varies two instruction factors over a 4 × 4 grid of 16 instructions:

  • Bun (color × spatial): “put the green bun into the first steamer basket on the left”
  • Pizza (spatial × object): “add mustard sauce to the pizza in the top left corner”
  • Cup (verb × color): “put the stirring spoon into the red cup”
